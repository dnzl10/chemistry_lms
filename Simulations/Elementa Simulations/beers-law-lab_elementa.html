Theory & Mathematical Principles

Beer-Lambert Law & Spectrophotometry Principles

Fundamental mathematical laws, photon attenuation equations, molar absorptivity, and calibration curve analytics.
The Beer-Lambert Law
The Beer-Lambert Law quantitatively relates the attenuation of light passing through a substance to the properties of the material. Absorbance (A) is directly proportional to the molar absorptivity (ε), the path length (b), and the molar concentration (c).
A = ε · b · c
Where:
• A = Absorbance (dimensionless unit / AU)
• ε = Molar absorptivity or extinction coefficient (M⁻¹·cm⁻¹)
• b = Optical path length (cm)
• c = Molar concentration of solute (M = mol/L)
Transmittance & Absorbance Relationship
Transmittance (T) is the fraction of incident light (I₀) that passes through the sample and emerges as transmitted light (I). Because light attenuation is an exponential decay process governed by photon absorption, absorbance is defined as the negative common logarithm of transmittance.
T = I / I₀  |  %T = (I / I₀) × 100%
A = −log₁₀(T) = log₁₀(I₀ / I) = 2 − log₁₀(%T)
A sample transmitting 100% of light has A = 0. A sample transmitting 10% has A = 1.0; 1% transmittance corresponds to A = 2.0.
Wavelength of Maximum Absorbance (λ max)
Every chemical species possesses a distinctive electronic absorption spectrum. The peak wavelength where molar absorptivity is greatest is known as λ max. Setting the light source to λ max maximizes analytical sensitivity and minimizes measurement error according to analytical spectrophotometry protocols.
ε(λ) = A / (b · c)  |  Measured at λ = λ_max
Complementary color rule: A solution absorbs the color complementary to its perceived transmitted color (e.g., cobalt nitrate is red/pink because it absorbs green light at ~549 nm).
Analytical Calibration & Standard Curves
In quantitative analytical chemistry, standard solutions of known concentrations are prepared and measured to construct a Calibration Curve. When absorbance is plotted against concentration, linear regression yields a straight line passing through the origin.
y = m · x + 0  ⟹  A = (ε · b) · c
The slope (m) equals ε · b. By measuring the absorbance of an unknown solution, its concentration is accurately determined: c_unknown = A_unknown / m.
Deviations and Limitations of Beer's Law
The linear relationship of Beer's Law is valid primarily for dilute solutions (typically ≤ 0.01 M). Deviations from linearity occur due to:
A > 1.5 ~ 2.0  ⟹  Non-linear detector response
• Chemical deviations: Solute-solvent association, dissociation, or dimerization at high concentration.
• Instrumental deviations: Polychromatic radiation (stray light) and detector saturation at very low transmittance.
Aqueous Dilution & Molarity Calculations
Solute concentration (M) is defined as moles of solute per liter of solution. When solvent is added to dilute a solution, total moles of solute remain conserved.
M = n / V  |  M₁ · V₁ = M₂ · V₂
Diluting a sample halves its absorbance according to Beer's law (since c is halved while path length b and absorptivity ε remain constant).
