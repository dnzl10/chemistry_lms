Question 1 of 5
In the reaction 2 H₂ + 1 O₂ → 2 H₂O, if you start with 8 molecules of H₂ and 5 molecules of O₂, how many H₂O molecules can be produced, and which reactant is in excess?
Explanation: Each 2 H₂ requires 1 O₂. To consume 8 H₂, 4 O₂ are needed. Since 5 O₂ are available, H₂ runs out first (limiting reactant). Exactly 8 H₂O molecules are formed, leaving 5 − 4 = 1 O₂ unreacted in excess.
Question 2 of 5
In the Haber-Bosch synthesis of ammonia (1 N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃), you mix 4 molecules of N₂ with 9 molecules of H₂. What will remain in the reaction vessel after the reaction goes to completion?
Explanation: 9 H₂ molecules can only react with 9 ÷ 3 = 3 N₂ molecules to produce 3 × 2 = 6 NH₃ molecules. H₂ is the limiting reactant, leaving 4 − 3 = 1 N₂ unreacted.
Question 3 of 5
Which statement accurately defines the 'limiting reactant' in a chemical reaction?
Explanation: The limiting reactant is determined by mole-to-coefficient ratios, not by initial mass alone. It is the reactant that is entirely consumed first, stopping the reaction and capping the theoretical yield.
Question 4 of 5
Consider the combustion of methane: 1 CH₄ + 2 O₂ → 1 CO₂ + 2 H₂O. If 5 molecules of CH₄ are mixed with 6 molecules of O₂, how many total molecules exist AFTER the reaction?
Explanation: 6 O₂ can combust 6 ÷ 2 = 3 CH₄ molecules. This yields 3 CO₂ + 6 H₂O = 9 product molecules. Additionally, 5 − 3 = 2 CH₄ molecules remain unreacted. The total count after reaction is 3 + 6 + 2 = 11 molecules.
Question 5 of 5
Why can the total number of molecules change during a chemical reaction (e.g. 2 H₂ + 1 O₂ = 3 molecules yielding only 2 H₂O molecules) without violating the Law of Conservation of Mass?
Explanation: In chemical reactions, individual atoms cannot be created or destroyed (mass is conserved: 4 H and 2 O atoms before and after). However, atoms recombine into different molecular arrangements, so the total number of independent molecules frequently changes.
Theory & Mathematical Principles

Chemical Stoichiometry & Reaction Yield Reference

Fundamental principles governing chemical proportions, limiting reactants, excess leftovers, and conservation laws.

1. The Law of Conservation of Mass

Formulated by Antoine Lavoisier, the Law of Conservation of Mass states that matter cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. The total mass of reactants must equal the total mass of products and leftovers:

Total Atoms Before = Total Atoms After
  • Every individual atomic nucleus is strictly conserved.
  • Chemical equations must be balanced by stoichiometric coefficients so that atom counts for each element match on both sides.

2. Limiting Reactant & Excess Reagent

Reactants are rarely supplied in exact stoichiometric ratios. The reaction proceeds only until one of the reactants is completely depleted:

  • Limiting Reactant: The reactant that yields the smallest amount of product based on stoichiometric mole ratios. It is completely consumed.
  • Excess Reactant: The reactant present in a quantity greater than necessary to react with the limiting reactant. Unreacted molecules remain as leftovers.

4. Theoretical Yield & Percent Yield

The theoretical yield is the maximum mass of product that could be formed from the given amounts of reactants, calculated from the limiting reactant:

Percent Yield = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) × 100%

In actual laboratory synthesis, percent yield is typically < 100% due to incomplete reactions, side reactions, or product loss during isolation.

5. Method for Determining the Limiting Reactant

Follow this standardized 3-step analytical procedure:

  • Step 1: Balance the chemical equation to identify stoichiometric coefficients.
  • Step 2: Divide the initial moles of each reactant by its stoichiometric coefficient: n / coefficient.
  • Step 3: The reactant with the lowest quotient is the limiting reactant. Use its value to compute product yield and subtract consumed amounts to find leftovers.
