Theory & Mathematical Principles

Theoretical Foundations & Thermodynamic Laws

Kinetic molecular theory, phase equilibria, interatomic potentials, and intermolecular forces.

Kinetic Molecular Theory

Matter is made of constantly moving particles. Temperature is directly proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of the particles:

⟨Ek⟩ = (3 / 2) • kB T

In solids, intermolecular forces hold particles in fixed lattice positions (vibrational motion). In liquids, particles slide past each other. In gases, kinetic energy overwhelms attractive forces entirely.

Ideal Gas Law

The state of a gas in a closed container is governed by the relation between pressure, volume, particle count, and absolute temperature:

P V = N kB T = n R T
  • P: Pressure exerted by molecular collisions against container walls
  • V: Volume accessible to the gas
  • T: Absolute temperature in Kelvin (K)

Phase Diagrams & Triple Point

A phase diagram maps the thermodynamically stable states as functions of Temperature and Pressure:

(dP / dT) = ΔH / (T • ΔV)
  • Triple Point: The unique combination of T and P where solid, liquid, and gas coexist in stable thermodynamic equilibrium.
  • Critical Point: Beyond this temperature and pressure, the distinction between liquid and gas vanishes, creating a supercritical fluid.

Lennard-Jones (12-6) Potential

Intermolecular interactions between neutral atoms are approximated by the Lennard-Jones potential function:

V(r) = 4ε • [ (σ / r)12 - (σ / r)6 ]
  • (σ/r)12: Strong Pauli repulsion from overlapping electron clouds
  • -(σ/r)6: Attractive London dispersion (van der Waals) forces
  • ε: Depth of the potential well (bond strength)

Anomalous Behavior of Water

Unlike almost all other substances, liquid water expands when freezing. As temperature drops below 4°C, water molecules organize into an open hexagonal lattice enforced by tetrahedral hydrogen bonding:

ρ(ice) ≈ 0.917 g/cm3 < ρ(liquid water) ≈ 1.000 g/cm3

Because solid ice is less dense than liquid water, ice floats on water, and the solid-liquid coexistence line on water's phase diagram has a negative slope (dP/dT < 0).

Latent Heat & Phase Transitions

During a phase change, heat added does not change the temperature; instead, it provides the latent heat required to overcome intermolecular potential energy:

Q = m • L

Lf: Latent heat of fusion (melting/freezing)
Lv: Latent heat of vaporization (boiling/condensing)
