Theory & Mathematical Principles

Theory & Formulas

Fundamental quantum mechanics, spectroscopy, and atomic structure principles governing the hydrogen atom.

Dalton & Thomson Models (1803–1904)
Dalton modeled atoms as solid indivisible spheres explaining stoichiometry, but with zero internal charge or spectral lines. Thomson discovered the electron and proposed the "Plum Pudding" model of uniform positive charge embedded with electrons, predicting a single oscillation frequency.
Dalton: Indivisible Sphere • Thomson: ω₀ = √(e² / 4πε₀ m R³)
Rutherford Catastrophe (1911)
Gold-foil alpha scattering revealed a compact central positive nucleus orbited by electrons. However, classical Maxwell-Larmor electrodynamics dictates that accelerating charges radiate continuous electromagnetic power, causing the electron to spiral into the nucleus in ~10⁻¹¹ s.
P = (e² a²) / (6πε₀ c³) ⇒ τ_collapse ≈ 10⁻¹¹ s (Classical Catastrophe)
Bohr Quantization Postulates (1913)
Niels Bohr resolved atomic instability by postulating discrete stationary orbits where orbital angular momentum is quantized in integer multiples of ℏ = h / 2π. Radiation is emitted or absorbed only when electrons jump between discrete stationary states.
L = m v r_n = n ℏ, r_n = n² a₀ (a₀ = 0.529 Å)
Rydberg Formula & Spectral Series
The wavelengths of all emitted spectral lines of atomic hydrogen are given precisely by the empirical Rydberg-Ritz formula, where R_H = 1.097373 × 10⁷ m⁻¹. Transitions ending on n₁=1 form the UV Lyman series; n₁=2 form the visible Balmer series.
1/λ = R_H (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²), ΔE = hν = hc/λ
de Broglie Matter Waves (1924)
Louis de Broglie proposed that particles possess wave characteristics with de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p. Bohr's quantum orbits represent constructive interference of standing electron waves wrapped around the circumference, requiring integer wavelengths 2π r = nλ.
λ_dB = h / p = h / (m v), 2π r_n = n λ_dB
Schrödinger Wave Mechanics (1926)
Erwin Schrödinger replaced deterministic planetary orbits with continuous 3D wavefunctions ψ(r, θ, φ) derived from the time-independent wave equation. The electron position is described by a probability density distribution P(r) = |ψ|².
Ĥψ = Eψ, P(r) = |ψ_nlm(r)|², E_n = −13.60 eV / n²
