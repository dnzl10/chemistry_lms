Theoretical Foundations of Blackbody Radiation

A blackbody is an idealized physical body that absorbs all incident electromagnetic radiation, regardless of frequency or angle of incidence. When in thermal equilibrium, it emits electromagnetic radiation termed blackbody radiation.

1. Planck's Radiation Law

In 1900, Max Planck derived the exact spectral power density distribution by postulating that energy is exchanged in discrete packets called quanta ($E = h u$):

B(λ, T) = (2π h c² / λ⁵) • [ 1 / (exp(hc / λkT) − 1) ]

Where $h = 6.626 imes 10^{-34} ext{ J}cdot ext{s}$, $c = 2.998 imes 10^8 ext{ m/s}$, and $k = 1.381 imes 10^{-23} ext{ J/K}$.

2. Wien's Displacement Law

By differentiating Planck's function and setting $dB/dlambda = 0$, we obtain the relationship between the peak wavelength and absolute temperature:

λ_peak • T = b ≈ 2897.8 µm • K (or 2.898 × 10⁻³ m • K)

As an object heats up, its peak emission shifts to shorter wavelengths (higher frequencies and photon energies).

3. Stefan-Boltzmann Law

Integrating Planck's law across all wavelengths from $0$ to $infty$ yields the total radiant power per unit surface area:

I = ∫₀^∞ B(λ, T) dλ = σ T⁴ (where σ = 5.67037 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴)

A doubling of temperature produces a $2^4 = 16$-fold increase in emitted radiant power!

4. The Ultraviolet Catastrophe

Classical electrodynamics (Rayleigh-Jeans law: $B_{RJ} = rac{2ckT}{lambda^4}$) assumed continuous energy modes, predicting infinite energy at short wavelengths ($lambda o 0$). Planck's quantum hypothesis resolved this paradox, launching modern quantum mechanics.
